
What are the most common security deposit deduction disputes in Indian rental flats and PGs? Give me a concise, room-by-room move-in inspection checklist (Bedroom, Bathroom, Kitchen, Living Area, Appliances/Electricals) covering the exact items tenants should photograph before moving in.
The biggest deposit disputes in India are usually about painting/cleaning, “damage” versus normal wear and tear, unpaid bills, missing items, and delayed or undocumented refunds. The strongest protection is a dated photo/video record plus a signed inventory and meter readings on move-in day.[1][2][3][4]
Common deduction disputes
DisputeWhat landlords often claimWhat tenants should establishPainting and repaintingFull repaint or “painting charges” after move-outExisting wall marks, peeling paint, stains, and nail holes were already present; ordinary fading is normal wear and tear. [4][5]CleaningFlat/PG was not cleaned or requires professional cleaningTake move-in photos of floors, bathrooms, kitchen, balconies, and storage; keep the agreement’s cleaning clause visible. [6][5]Damage vs wear and tearBroken fittings, chipped tiles, damaged furniture, holes in wallsPhotograph every pre-existing defect close-up; distinguish accidental/tenant damage from ordinary use. [3][7][8]Utility arrearsElectricity, water, gas, maintenance, society duesPhotograph all meter readings and record the last paid bill; clarify in writing which bills the tenant must pay. [2][3][6]Missing or broken inventoryMissing keys, remotes, curtains, appliances, furnitureMake a signed annexure listing every item, its condition, serial number where applicable, and photos. [6][5][9]Appliance conditionAC, geyser, fridge, washing machine, chimney or hob was damagedRecord make/model, working condition, existing scratches/dents, and test each appliance before signing. [6][9]Deposit refund delayLandlord withholds money pending repairs or billsAsk for an itemized deduction list with bills/photos; keep rent receipts, agreement, handover messages, and move-out photos. [1][10][4]PG-specific issuesDamaged bed/mattress, cupboard, fan, shared-area cleaning, lock/key issuesInspect the individual room and shared facilities separately; get the PG operator to sign the room condition sheet. [11][12]
A landlord may generally deduct verified unpaid rent, tenant-responsible utility bills, and actual repair costs for damage beyond normal use; vague, round-figure deductions without evidence are much harder to justify.[3][5]
Before you photograph
* Take a continuous walkthrough video of every room, then take close-up still photos of defects.
* Ensure photos show the date; if your phone does not timestamp visibly, keep the original files and send them by email/WhatsApp to the owner the same day.
* Photograph both wide shots and close-ups—wide for location, close-up for the defect.
* Do not rely only on verbal assurances; ask the owner/PG manager to sign or reply in writing: “Condition noted as on [date].”
* Record electricity, water, and gas meter readings, with a photo of each meter.[6][13]
Bedroom
Photograph before moving in:
* Full room from each corner, including ceiling, floor, and all walls.
* Walls: stains, dampness/seepage, cracks, peeling paint, holes, hooks, and existing marks.
* Ceiling: water stains, mould, cracks, and fan condition.
* Floor and skirting: tiles, wooden flooring, scratches, broken tiles, gaps, and damp patches.
* Door and lock: door surface, handle, hinges, key, latch, and whether it locks properly.
* Windows and grill: glass cracks, mesh damage, handles, curtains/rods, and water seepage.
* Bed: frame, headboard, legs, mattress stains/tears, and underside if accessible.
* Wardrobe/almirah: doors, hinges, handles, shelves, drawers, locks, and interior scratches.
* Study table/chair, if provided: surface, drawers, wheels, and stability.
* Fan, tube light/LED, switchboard, and every socket—test that they work.
* Any existing insect/pest damage, electrical exposed wiring, or safety issue.
For a PG, also photograph the mattress, pillow, bedsheet condition, locker/cupboard, and the room number/door plate.[11][12]
Bathroom
Photograph before moving in:
* Wide shot of the entire bathroom, including ceiling and floor.
* Walls and ceiling: seepage, mould, peeling paint, cracked tiles, and grout condition.
* Floor: broken tiles, uneven surfaces, drainage slope, and standing-water issue.
* WC/toilet seat: cracks, seat condition, flush working, and cistern.
* Washbasin: cracks, taps, drain flow, and under-sink leakage.
* Shower area: showerhead, mixer, geyser, bucket/mug, and drainage.
* Taps and pipes: leaks, rust, loose fittings, and water pressure.
* Geyser: brand/model if visible, switches, indicator light, and any leakage; do not test excessively if it appears unsafe.
* Exhaust fan/window: working condition, grille, and ventilation.
* Mirror, shelf, towel rod, door, latch, and lock.
* Drain covers and any existing blockage or foul smell.
Test water flow, hot water, flush, and drainage during the inspection; photograph or video the result.[9][6]
Kitchen
Photograph before moving in:
* Wide shots of counters, cabinets, walls, ceiling, and floor.
* Modular kitchen/cabinets: hinges, handles, shelves, drawers, water damage, and interior condition.
* Countertop: burns, cracks, chips, stains, and loose edges.
* Sink: basin condition, tap, drain, leakage under the sink, and waste-pipe condition.
* Walls near stove/sink: oil stains, tiles, seepage, and existing damage.
* Chimney/hood: condition, filters, and whether it switches on.
* Hob/stove: burners, knobs, ignition, gas-pipe connection, and cylinder/regulator if provided.
* Gas cylinder and regulator: condition, connection, and any leakage concern; do not use an open flame to test.
* Water purifier/RO, if provided: unit, filter condition, leakage, and power connection.
* Exhaust fan/window and ventilation.
* Electrical sockets near the counter, especially for mixer, kettle, microwave, and refrigerator.
* Any provided utensils, containers, or kitchen appliances—photograph each item and note condition.
For a PG with a shared kitchen, photograph the assigned shelf/cupboard, shared appliances, and any existing damage to communal equipment.[6][9]
Living area
Photograph before moving in:
* Wide shots from multiple angles, including ceiling, walls, and floor.
* Walls: nail holes, stains, dampness, cracks, peeling paint, and existing artwork marks.
* Ceiling: seepage, cracks, mould, and light fittings.
* Floor: scratches, cracked tiles, carpet/rug condition, and skirting.
* Sofa/chairs: tears, stains, sagging cushions, broken legs, and frame condition.
* Coffee table, dining table, chairs, shelves, and any other furniture.
* Curtains, blinds, curtain rods, and window condition.
* Balcony: railing, flooring, drain, door, lock, grill, and any stored items.
* Main door: peephole, chain, handle, lock, keys, and door frame.
* Lights, fans, switchboards, and sockets—test each one.
* Intercom, doorbell, CCTV access, parking slot, and society amenities if relevant.
* Any pre-existing damage near the entrance, corridor, or common areas that could later be attributed to you.
Appliances and electricals
Photograph before moving in:
* Every appliance’s front, back, sides, and control panel: fridge, washing machine, microwave, oven, AC, geyser, water purifier, chimney, mixer, iron, and television.
* Make, model, and serial-number labels where visible.
* Existing dents, scratches, rust, cracks, missing knobs, and damaged cords.
* Test operation: AC cooling, fridge cooling/light, washing-machine cycle, microwave heating, geyser heating, chimney suction, and TV signal.
* Photograph remote controls, chargers, adapters, and accessories; list whether each is provided.
* Switchboards: broken plates, loose sockets, burn marks, and exposed wiring.
* Fans, tube lights, LED panels, and emergency lights—test all switches.
* Electrical meter, water meter, and gas meter readings; photograph the display clearly.
* WiFi router, if provided: condition, adapter, and whether it is included in the agreement.
* Any appliance already not working—make this explicit in the signed inventory rather than merely photographing it.[9][6]
Make it dispute-proof
Create a one-page inventory with columns for item, location, condition, existing damage, photos/video reference, and owner’s remarks. Send the photos, video, inventory, and meter readings to the owner or PG manager on move-in day, and retain the acknowledgement.[5][12][6]
At move-out, repeat the exact same process in the same order. This makes it easy to show what changed during your tenancy—and to challenge deductions for issues that existed before you moved in.[4][3][5]

In Indian residential tenancy practices (including the Model Tenancy Act principles), what clearly counts as normal wear and tear versus tenant-caused chargeable damage? Give a structured comparison table with 8–10 concrete examples.
Under the Model Tenancy Act, 2021 principles, the premises must be returned in substantially the same condition as at the start of tenancy, except for normal wear and tear. Ordinary ageing, minor scuffs, and faded paint are generally not chargeable; negligence, misuse, unauthorised alterations, and damage beyond ordinary use can be.[16][17][18]
The dividing line
Normal wear and tearTenant-caused chargeable damageGradual deterioration from ordinary, careful useDamage caused by negligence, misuse, accident, or deliberate actionExpected ageing of paint, fittings, flooring, and appliancesA specific defect that would not have occurred through normal livingUsually the landlord’s cost, unless the agreement validly allocates it otherwiseMay be repaired by the landlord and recovered from the deposit, with evidence of the cost. [16][17]
The MTA’s Second Schedule places structural repairs, whitewashing, painting of doors/windows, and major plumbing/electrical wiring with the landlord; tenants are responsible for certain periodic and minor repairs, such as tap washers, drain cleaning, switches/sockets, door knobs/locks, fly-nets, and glass panels, subject to the agreement. If a tenant fails to do repairs assigned to them, the landlord may carry them out and deduct the documented cost from the deposit.[17][19][20]

Important qualification
A deduction should be tied to a specific, evidenced repair, not a generic “painting,” “cleaning,” or “damages” charge. Under the MTA framework, if the tenant fails to carry out repairs for which they are responsible, the landlord may perform them and deduct the cost incurred from the security deposit; the tenant must pay any excess beyond the deposit within one month of notice.[20][17]
The exact result can change if your written rent agreement validly allocates particular repairs differently, but it cannot ordinarily convert normal deterioration into tenant damage.[18][16][17]
